I stay after events. When the audience leaves and the organizers start
stacking chairs, that's usually when the best photograph of the night happens.
After covering thirty college events, here's what that habit taught me about
telling stories — in photos, and in software.

## The event is not the story

The schedule says the story is the chief guest and the prize ceremony. It
never is. The story is the volunteer asleep on the sound console at 11pm, the
first-year rehearsing in a stairwell. Coverage that follows the schedule
produces documentation; coverage that follows tension produces narrative.

## Timing is a skill you can practice

Thirty events teaches you when moments happen: the genuine laugh is the
second one, after the posed one. The same instinct transfers to product work —
users' real reaction is the second one too, after the polite one.

## Constraints make the style

One camera, one lens, bad indoor light — every event, the same constraints.
Style isn't what you add; it's what your constraints force you to repeat until
it becomes recognizable.

## Why this is on a developer's blog

Because the portfolio you're reading is built on the same idea: follow the
tension (the problem), time the reveal (the launch), respect the constraints
(the budget). Storytelling isn't a separate skill from building — it's the
same skill pointed at people instead of machines.
